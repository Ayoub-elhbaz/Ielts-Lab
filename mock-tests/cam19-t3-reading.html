Passage 1
The lost art of the lighthouse keeper

For more than two centuries, one of the most demanding and isolated occupations in the world was also one of the most quietly essential. The lighthouse keeper — the person responsible for tending the great lamps that warned ships away from rocks, reefs and shoals — occupied a role that combined technical skill, physical endurance and an almost monastic tolerance for solitude. Today the profession has all but disappeared, made obsolete by automation, and the knowledge that keepers once carried has largely vanished with them.

The work was never glamorous, though it has often been romanticised since. A keeper's primary duty was to ensure that the light never failed. In the era before electricity, this meant tending an oil lamp through the night, trimming its wick at regular intervals to keep the flame bright and steady, and constantly cleaning the soot that the burning oil deposited on the glass of the lantern room. The great lenses that focused the light — known as Fresnel lenses, after the French engineer who designed them — had to be polished daily, for even a thin film of salt or grime could weaken the beam that ships depended on.

Beyond the light itself, keepers maintained the entire station. They kept meticulous logs of weather, passing vessels and any unusual events. They operated fog signals, which in the days before electronic horns might be powered by compressed air or even, in earlier times, by firing a cannon at intervals. At remote rock stations, far from any shore, keepers also had to be their own mechanics, cooks, and, when necessary, medics. A keeper who fell ill might wait weeks for relief if storms prevented a supply boat from landing.

Isolation was the defining feature of the life. The most exposed stations, built on bare rocks battered by the open sea, were known in the British service as 'rock stations', and a posting to one was both a mark of trust and a kind of sentence. Keepers at such places might be cut off from the mainland for months at a stretch. They typically worked in teams of three, a number arrived at after a notorious eighteenth-century incident in which a station had been staffed by only two men. When one of them died, the survivor — fearing he would be accused of murder if he buried the body — kept the corpse beside him until relief arrived weeks later, by which point he had been driven half mad. After that, three keepers became the standard, so that no one would ever again be left alone with a single companion.

Despite the hardship, the role attracted a particular kind of person, and many keepers served for decades. The work suited those who valued routine, self-reliance and the company of the sea over that of people. Families sometimes lived at the shore stations, and the children of keepers occasionally followed their parents into the service, creating dynasties that spanned generations. There was pride in the work, and a strong professional culture built around the absolute reliability the job demanded. A light that failed, even once, could cost dozens of lives.

The decline of the profession was gradual and then sudden. Electrification, beginning in the late nineteenth century, removed the constant labour of tending oil lamps. Automatic timers, remote monitoring and, eventually, satellite positioning made it possible for a single control centre to oversee dozens of lights without anyone present at any of them. One by one, stations were automated and their keepers withdrawn. In most maritime nations, the last manned lighthouse was decommissioned somewhere between the 1980s and the early 2000s. The very last keepers often spoke of the strange experience of training the machines that would replace them.

What was lost when the keepers left is harder to measure than what was saved. Automation is cheaper and, by most measures, more reliable; modern navigation systems have made even the lights themselves less essential than they once were, and some have been switched off entirely. But the keepers possessed a kind of local knowledge that no sensor records — an intimate understanding of how a particular stretch of coast behaved in particular weathers, of the moods of the sea around their rock. Sailors in distress had sometimes been saved not by the light but by a keeper who noticed something wrong and raised the alarm.

Many former lighthouses now serve as museums, holiday lets or private homes, their lantern rooms dark or lit automatically. The keepers themselves have become figures of nostalgia, celebrated in books and films that tend to emphasise the romance and overlook the tedium. Yet the profession deserves to be remembered for what it actually was: not a poetic exile, but a skilled and disciplined form of public service, carried out for generations by people whose entire purpose was to be reliable on behalf of strangers they would never meet.

Passage 2
How plants tell the time

AIn 1729 the French astronomer Jean-Jacques d'Ortous de Mairan noticed something peculiar about a plant on his windowsill. The mimosa, whose leaves opened in daylight and folded shut at night, continued to follow this rhythm even when he placed it in a cupboard in constant darkness. The leaves still opened in the morning and closed in the evening, despite having no way of detecting whether it was day or night. De Mairan had stumbled upon one of the most fundamental features of life on Earth: an internal biological clock, ticking away independently of the outside world.

BAlmost three centuries later, the mechanism behind that observation has been worked out in remarkable detail, and it turns out that plants are among the most sophisticated timekeepers in nature. Like animals, plants possess what biologists call a circadian rhythm — an internal cycle of roughly twenty-four hours that governs a vast range of activities. In plants, the clock controls when leaves and petals open, when scent is released, when certain genes are switched on and off, and even when the plant is most receptive to particular wavelengths of light. The word circadian comes from the Latin circa diem, meaning 'about a day', and the 'about' is important: the internal cycle is close to twenty-four hours but not exactly, which is why it must be reset daily against the real world.

CThat resetting is achieved through environmental cues, of which the most important is light. Specialised light-sensitive proteins in the plant's cells detect the shift from darkness to dawn and nudge the internal clock back into alignment with the solar day. Temperature plays a secondary role; the cooler nights and warmer days of a normal cycle help reinforce the rhythm. A plant deprived of all such cues, like de Mairan's mimosa in its cupboard, will keep running on its internal clock alone, but the rhythm will gradually drift out of step with the real world — proof that the clock is internal but normally corrected from outside.

DWhy should a plant need to know the time at all? The answer lies in the advantage of anticipation. A plant that can predict sunrise, rather than merely react to it, can have its photosynthetic machinery ready the moment light arrives, wasting none of the precious early daylight. Experiments have shown that plants whose internal clocks are artificially set to match their environment grow larger and accumulate more carbohydrate than identical plants whose clocks are deliberately mismatched. The clock, in other words, is not a curiosity but a competitive tool, honed by natural selection because good timekeeping translates directly into more energy and faster growth.

EThe clock also governs defence. Many plants are attacked by insects that themselves follow daily rhythms, feeding most heavily at particular times. Researchers studying the much-used laboratory plant Arabidopsis found that it ramps up production of defensive chemicals in anticipation of the hours when caterpillars are most likely to feed. When the plant's clock was disrupted so that its defences no longer aligned with the insects' feeding schedule, the plants suffered significantly more damage. The plant was, in effect, scheduling its immune response in advance, like a shopkeeper locking the doors before the hour when thieves are known to operate.

FPerhaps the most striking demonstration of plant timekeeping concerns pollination. Some flowers open and release their scent only at the specific times of day when their preferred pollinators are active. Certain night-flowering species, pollinated by moths, stay tightly closed and scentless through the day and then, as dusk approaches, open and begin to emit their fragrance — not in response to darkness, which has not yet fully fallen, but in anticipation of it, driven by the internal clock. The flower and the insect, over evolutionary time, have synchronised their schedules to mutual benefit.

GUnderstanding the plant clock is not merely of academic interest. Agriculture depends on plants growing in places and seasons far removed from where their clocks originally evolved, and a clock poorly matched to local day length can reduce yields. Plant breeders have begun to select varieties whose internal timing suits particular latitudes, and researchers hope that a deeper understanding of the clock's genetics will allow crops to be tuned for the lengthening or shortening days they will actually experience. There is also interest in how the clock will cope as climate change shifts the relationship between temperature and day length — two cues that have always moved together but may increasingly diverge.

HWhat began as an idle observation of a plant in a cupboard has thus grown into a field with serious practical stakes. The deeper lesson of de Mairan's mimosa is that even organisms without brains, eyes or nervous systems can keep time, anticipate the future and act on that anticipation. The plant on the windowsill was not simply responding to the world around it. It was, in its own silent way, predicting it.

Passage 3
The trouble with measuring happiness

For most of its history as a discipline, economics concerned itself with things that could be counted: incomes, prices, output, employment. Happiness was left to philosophers and poets. Over the past few decades, however, a growing number of economists have argued that this division of labour was a mistake — that the ultimate purpose of economic activity is human wellbeing, and that a science measuring everything except wellbeing has lost sight of why it exists. The result has been the rise of what is sometimes called the economics of happiness, a field that attempts something its founders would once have considered absurd: to measure how good people's lives actually feel.

The motivation is easy to understand. For most of the twentieth century, economic success was equated with growth in gross domestic product, the total monetary value of goods and services a country produces. GDP is a powerful and convenient measure, but it was never designed to capture welfare, and its limitations are by now well known. It counts a traffic jam, an oil spill and a divorce as economic activity because money changes hands, while ignoring leisure, clean air, good health and the unpaid work that holds households together. A country could, in principle, grow steadily richer by the GDP measure while its citizens grew steadily more miserable.

The obvious alternative is simply to ask people how they feel. Happiness researchers do exactly this, typically through large surveys in which respondents rate their satisfaction with life on a numerical scale. These subjective wellbeing measures have produced some robust and widely replicated findings. Within any given country, richer people report higher life satisfaction than poorer ones, as common sense would predict. But across countries and over time the picture is stranger. Above a certain level of income — enough to meet basic needs comfortably — additional national wealth seems to buy surprisingly little extra happiness, a puzzle first noted by the economist Richard Easterlin and named the Easterlin paradox after him.

The paradox has been the subject of fierce and unresolved debate. One camp argues that it is largely an illusion produced by the way happiness is measured: a survey scale that runs from one to ten cannot register improvement indefinitely, because once a population is clustered near the top of the scale, no further improvement can show up in the numbers. On this view, the apparent flattening of happiness is an artefact of the ruler, not a fact about human experience. Another camp maintains that the paradox is real and reflects a genuine feature of human psychology: that people judge their circumstances relative to those around them, so that as everyone grows richer together, no one feels better off.

This second explanation, if correct, has uncomfortable implications. It suggests that much of the effort a wealthy society pours into further growth is, in terms of actual wellbeing, self-cancelling — a race in which everyone runs faster but no one moves forward, because each person's gain is measured against everyone else's. Beyond a certain point, the pursuit of more would be not merely ineffective but structurally incapable of delivering what it promises. Critics counter that this underestimates how much people value the genuine new possibilities wealth brings, and that comparison with others is only one of many psychological processes at work.

Even setting aside the Easterlin debate, the project faces a deeper conceptual problem: it is not clear that the thing being measured is a single thing at all. Philosophers have long distinguished two notions of a good life. One is hedonic — the moment-to-moment balance of pleasant and unpleasant feelings. The other is eudaimonic, a concept inherited from Aristotle, concerned with whether a life is meaningful and well lived, regardless of how it feels at any instant. A person doing difficult, demanding work might score low on momentary pleasure while rating their life as deeply worthwhile. Which of these is happiness? Surveys that ask about 'life satisfaction' may capture the second; surveys that sample momentary mood capture the first; and the two do not always move together.

These difficulties have not stopped governments from taking the measures seriously. Several countries now collect national wellbeing statistics alongside their economic data, and a few have used them to guide policy directly. The kingdom of Bhutan famously adopted 'Gross National Happiness' as an explicit goal of government. Such initiatives are easy to mock, and skeptics point out that a single happiness number is far too crude to settle real policy trade-offs. Yet defenders argue that even an imperfect measure of wellbeing is better than none, and that the act of measuring something signals what a society chooses to value.

Perhaps the most defensible conclusion is also the most modest. Happiness data are not precise enough to replace traditional economic indicators, and the concept they attempt to capture is too contested to be reduced to a single figure. But they serve as a valuable corrective — a reminder that the numbers economists have always trusted were only ever a proxy for something else, and that the proxy should never be mistaken for the goal. The economics of happiness may not yet be able to tell us exactly how to make people's lives go better. Its real achievement has been to insist that this was always the question worth asking.
