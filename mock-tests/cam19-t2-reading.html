Passage 1
The rediscovery of saffron in England

For nearly four centuries, the small market town of Saffron Walden in eastern England took its name from a crop that had once been one of the most valuable commodities in Europe. Crocus sativus, the autumn-flowering plant whose dried red stigmas produce the spice saffron, was cultivated across the fields of Essex from the late fourteenth century until the early nineteenth, when changing tastes, cheaper imports and rising labour costs caused the industry to vanish entirely. By 1820, the last grower had given up, and within a generation, the knowledge of how to plant, harvest and dry the crocus had been almost completely lost.

Saffron is famously labour-intensive. Each flower produces only three crimson stigmas, which must be picked by hand within hours of the bloom opening, then dried with great care to preserve both colour and flavour. Roughly 150,000 flowers are needed to make a single kilogram of the dried spice. In the medieval economy, where labour was cheap and saffron was used in everything from medicine and dyeing to the kitchens of the wealthy, the equation made sense. By the nineteenth century, with industrial wages rising and Spanish and Iranian saffron arriving by ship at a fraction of the cost, English saffron had become an impossible business.

The story might have ended there but for a retired Essex farmer named David Smale. In 2006, Smale, who had grown up hearing his grandfather speak of the saffron fields, began to wonder whether the crop could be brought back. He started with a small plot of around 200 corms — the bulb-like underground stems from which the crocus grows — sourced from a specialist nursery in the Netherlands. The first harvest yielded enough saffron to fill a single small jar. It was, by any commercial measure, a failure. But the spice was unmistakably present, and its quality, Smale insisted, was extraordinary.

What followed was less a business plan than a quiet experiment. Smale planted more corms each year and kept careful notes on soil, weather and yield. He discovered that the chalky, well-drained soils of Essex were, as the medieval growers had known, almost ideal. He also discovered that modern conditions presented problems his predecessors had not faced. Slugs and field mice, controlled in earlier centuries by intensive farming around the crocus beds, devastated his early plantings. Unusually wet autumns rotted the corms in the ground. It took nearly five years before Smale produced a harvest large enough to sell at a local farmers' market.

Word of the rediscovery spread slowly. A handful of restaurants in London began ordering small quantities of the English-grown spice, drawn by its remarkable freshness — saffron loses much of its aroma within a year of drying, and imported saffron is often two or three years old by the time it reaches British kitchens. A small magazine article in 2012 brought enquiries from across the country, and Smale found himself, somewhat reluctantly, training other would-be growers. Within a decade, there were eight saffron producers operating in England, most of them on plots of less than two acres.

The economics, however, remained challenging. English saffron sells for between £30 and £40 per gram, more than ten times the price of the imported product. Customers willing to pay such prices are, inevitably, a small market: high-end restaurants, specialist food shops and a growing number of consumers who treat saffron the way some treat single-estate olive oil or wine from a particular vineyard. Most growers earn modest supplementary incomes rather than full livings. Smale himself, now in his seventies, has never given up his other agricultural work.

What the revival has produced, perhaps more interestingly than any commercial product, is a slow recovery of practical knowledge. Researchers from the University of Essex have begun documenting the techniques the new growers are developing — when to lift the corms for storage, how deeply to plant them, which pests respond to which deterrents. Much of this information existed only in fragments in medieval and early modern farming manuals, and even there it was often vague or unreliable. The current growers are, in effect, reconstructing an agricultural tradition from the ground up.

Whether English saffron will become more than a curiosity remains uncertain. The crop will probably never again cover whole landscapes as it once did, and competing with low-cost imports for the mass market is not realistically possible. But the revival has demonstrated something that the spice trade had seemingly disproved: that a luxury product, made well and made nearby, can sustain itself even in an age of cheap global shipping. For the small town that gave the spice its English name, that is a quietly satisfying outcome.

Passage 2
Why cities are getting quieter

AAsk anyone who lives in a major city to describe the sound of their neighbourhood, and the answer will usually involve some version of the same complaint: it is too loud. Traffic, construction, sirens, neighbours, the rumble of underground trains — urban noise is one of the most consistent grievances of city living, and one of the most well-established environmental health hazards of modern life. Chronic exposure to noise above 55 decibels has been linked in numerous studies to elevated rates of cardiovascular disease, sleep disturbance and cognitive impairment in children. Public health agencies in several countries have begun to treat persistent noise as a problem comparable in seriousness to air pollution. Until quite recently, the situation seemed to be growing steadily worse.

BYet measurements taken in cities across Europe and North America over the past fifteen years tell a different story. Average daytime noise levels in central London, Paris and New York have fallen by between two and four decibels since 2010 — a drop that sounds small, but which represents a significant reduction in perceived loudness, since the decibel scale is logarithmic. A reduction of three decibels corresponds to roughly halving the sound energy reaching the ear. The trend, while uneven, is real, and the explanations behind it tell us a good deal about how cities are changing.

CThe single largest factor is the quiet revolution in vehicle technology. Electric and hybrid cars produce significantly less noise than petrol or diesel vehicles, particularly at urban speeds below thirty kilometres per hour, where engine sound dominates over the noise of tyres on the road. As electric vehicles have moved from a small minority of the urban fleet to a substantial share in many European cities, the cumulative effect on ambient sound has been measurable. Paris, which has actively encouraged electric taxis and delivery vehicles, has recorded some of the steepest declines.

DRoad surfaces have also changed. So-called low-noise asphalt, which contains air pockets that absorb tyre noise rather than reflecting it, has been installed on increasing numbers of urban roads. The material costs slightly more than conventional asphalt and tends to wear out faster, but cities have judged the trade-off worthwhile. A 2019 study in Stockholm found that resurfacing a single arterial road with low-noise asphalt reduced average traffic noise along it by 4.5 decibels — a change residents described as transformative.

EA third factor is rarely mentioned because it is harder to see: the decline of certain noisy activities altogether. The shift to remote and hybrid working has reduced rush-hour traffic in many city centres. Shopping has migrated online, which has thinned the volume of delivery vehicles operating in dense retail districts even as it has added them to suburban streets. Construction itself, while not quieter, is more often subject to local regulations that confine the noisiest work to limited hours. None of these changes was undertaken for the sake of acoustic comfort, but each has had acoustic consequences.

FNot everyone agrees that the headline figures tell the whole story. Critics point out that average noise levels conceal large variations across the city. The very streets that have benefited most from electric vehicles and resurfacing tend to be in wealthier districts; major arterial roads, freight corridors and the neighbourhoods around airports and rail depots have seen little change, and sometimes increases. The acoustic divide, some researchers argue, is widening — and the people most exposed to harmful noise are disproportionately those least able to move.

GThere are also concerns about a more subtle phenomenon. Electric vehicles, precisely because they are so quiet at low speeds, pose a documented safety risk to pedestrians, particularly visually impaired ones. Many jurisdictions now require electric vehicles to emit an artificial sound when travelling below a certain speed. The sounds were initially modelled on conventional engine noise, but research suggests that other patterns — softer, more variable, more like the natural environment — would be both safer and less stressful for residents. How quiet a city should be, in other words, is not a question with an obvious answer.

HWhat does seem clear is that the assumption of inevitable acoustic decline — the sense that cities must necessarily grow louder as they grow larger — has been disproved by the recent data. Quietness, like air quality or green space, turns out to be something cities can actively cultivate, and many are beginning to do so deliberately. Paris has designated 'tranquil zones' in residential districts where through-traffic is restricted; Barcelona has experimented with closing entire blocks to vehicles during certain hours. The results, while modest, are encouraging. A quieter city is not a fantasy, and it does not require abandoning urban life — only paying attention to what the city sounds like, and choosing differently.

Passage 3
The unsettled science of animal play

Few aspects of animal behaviour are at once so obvious and so theoretically troublesome as play. Anyone who has watched a litter of puppies wrestling, a young chimpanzee swinging from a branch in mock alarm, or a pair of crows passing a stick between them in mid-air, has witnessed something that resembles what humans do when we play. Yet for biologists, this resemblance has long been more difficult than illuminating. Behaviour that consumes energy, exposes the animal to risk, and does not directly serve survival or reproduction is precisely the kind of behaviour that natural selection ought, in theory, to eliminate. That it has not done so — that play appears in mammals, birds, some reptiles and even, in disputed cases, certain fish and invertebrates — suggests either that the textbook framework is missing something, or that play serves some function we have failed to identify.

The most enduring explanation, advanced in different forms since the late nineteenth century, is that play is preparation. Through chasing, pouncing, mock-fighting and the manipulation of objects, young animals are thought to develop the motor skills, social competence and tactical knowledge they will need as adults. The hypothesis has obvious intuitive appeal: a kitten stalking a moving piece of string today is rehearsing the predator it will become tomorrow. But intuitive appeal is not evidence, and when researchers have tried to test the prediction directly, the results have been inconsistent. Studies of meerkats, for example, found no clear correlation between the amount of mock-fighting an individual engaged in as a pup and its success in real conflicts as an adult. Similar findings have emerged from work on rats and several primate species. If play is rehearsal, the rehearsal does not appear to be very thorough.

A more recent line of thinking, associated particularly with the comparative psychologist Marc Bekoff, holds that the function of play is not skill-building at all, but the cultivation of behavioural flexibility. Animals that play, in this view, are not so much preparing for predictable adult tasks as learning to cope with the unexpected — to recover from being knocked off balance, to adjust to a sudden change in their partner's behaviour, to switch tactics when an approach fails. The capacity to respond creatively to novel situations is, on this account, what play develops; and the cost of acquiring such a capacity through real experience would be prohibitive. Better, evolutionarily, to practise on a sibling than on a predator.

This emotional dimension of play has become a particularly active area of investigation. Several mammals, including rats, have been shown to produce vocalisations during play that closely resemble laughter in their acoustic structure, and that disappear when the animal is stressed. Whether these sounds constitute genuine positive emotion in the human sense is philosophically contested, but the parallel is striking enough that researchers now use them as a measure of welfare in laboratory rodents. If an animal stops making its play-calls, something is wrong.

What complicates all of this is that play itself is harder to define than it might seem. The standard criteria — that the behaviour is voluntary, repeated, lacks immediate function, and is performed in a relaxed state — exclude some activities that seem like play and include others that do not. A young dolphin pushing a turtle around the surface of a bay for hours probably qualifies under any reasonable definition; what about a single octopus repeatedly pulling and releasing a floating pill bottle, as has been recorded in captivity? The behaviour fits the criteria, but the cognitive machinery behind it is so different from that of a mammal that calling it the same thing may be more misleading than helpful.

Some researchers have responded by treating play not as a single phenomenon but as a family of related ones — locomotor play (running, jumping for no reason), object play (manipulating items), and social play (mock-fighting and chase games) — each potentially serving different functions in different species. This approach captures real differences but gives up on a unified explanation. If play is many things, then asking 'why do animals play?' may be like asking 'why do animals eat?': the answer depends entirely on which animal and which behaviour.

Critics of the recent emphasis on emotion and flexibility have argued that the field is in danger of substituting attractive ideas for testable ones. The notion that animals play for fun, while almost certainly true in some sense, is not a scientific claim until 'fun' is operationally defined and measurable. Yet the more conservative alternatives — that play is purely a by-product of energy surplus, or an evolutionary accident in species with prolonged development — have struggled to explain the sheer diversity of forms play takes.

Where this leaves the question is, perhaps appropriately, unresolved. Play is among the few animal behaviours that researchers describe with words borrowed almost wholesale from human experience: relish, exuberance, joy. Whether this borrowing reflects a genuine continuity between human and animal minds, or merely an unavoidable limit of human language describing non-human things, is a question that biology, on its own, may not be equipped to answer.
