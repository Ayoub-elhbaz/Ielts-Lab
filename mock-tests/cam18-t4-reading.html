The Domestication of Dogs: From Wolf to Companion

AAmong the most significant and enduring partnerships in human prehistory is the relationship between Homo sapiens and the domestic dog, Canis lupus familiaris. Archaeological evidence and advances in ancient DNA analysis have together transformed our understanding of when, where, and how grey wolves were first brought into close association with human communities. Once assumed to be a single domestication event tied to the rise of settled agriculture, the process is now understood to be considerably more complex, potentially involving multiple independent episodes across different geographic regions and spanning a period far earlier than previously supposed.

BThe earliest securely dated dog remains come from burial sites in Europe and the Near East. A mandible recovered from the Bonn-Oberkassel site in Germany, dated to approximately 14,200 years before present, is widely accepted as representing a domestic dog rather than a wild wolf on the basis of its reduced dentition and shortened snout — morphological features produced by generations of selective breeding. A separate specimen from the Goyet Cave in Belgium, initially proposed as a proto-dog dating to 36,000 years before present, has attracted more controversy: while its cranial dimensions deviate significantly from those of contemporary wolves, it remains unclear whether this reflects deliberate domestication or localised environmental pressures that produced convergent morphological change without sustained human management.

CGenetic studies have substantially complicated the archaeological record. A landmark analysis published in Science in 2016 by researchers at the University of Oxford and Trinity College Dublin compared the mitochondrial DNA of 59 ancient dog specimens with that of modern dogs and wolves across Eurasia. The data indicated a pattern inconsistent with a single ancestral population: Eastern and Western dog lineages appeared to diverge from distinct wolf populations, with the split occurring at different times. The authors concluded that dogs may have been domesticated independently in both East Asia and Europe, with subsequent population movements and interbreeding obscuring the original signal in modern genomic data. A rival hypothesis, advanced by Greger Larson and colleagues, holds that a single domestication event in Central Asia remains more parsimonious given the available evidence, though this interpretation continues to be actively contested.

DThe functional role of dogs in early human societies is a subject of considerable speculation, though indirect evidence provides some basis for inference. Isotopic analysis of skeletal remains from burial contexts in which dogs and humans were interred together suggests that, in some populations, dogs were fed diets closely matching those of their human companions — an observation consistent with a close domestic relationship rather than opportunistic scavenging. Cave art from the Arabian Peninsula, dating to roughly 8,000 years before present and described by archaeologist Maria Guagnin of the Max Planck Institute for the Science of Human History, depicts coordinated hunting scenes in which leashed or directed dogs assist human hunters in driving prey, representing the earliest known visual record of working dogs.

ESelective pressures during domestication appear to have produced not merely morphological but also significant neurological and behavioural changes. Research conducted at Duke University's Canine Cognition Center has demonstrated that domestic dogs possess an unusual capacity to follow human communicative gestures — particularly pointing — that exceeds both that of chimpanzees and that of hand-raised wolves given equivalent socialisation. Dogs spontaneously orient towards the human gaze and use it as a cue for locating concealed resources, a behaviour absent in wolves raised under identical conditions. These findings suggest that domestication selectively favoured individuals with heightened sensitivity to human social signals, and that this cognitive trait may have been subject to rapid genetic fixation over relatively few generations.

FThe timing of dog domestication relative to the transition from hunter-gatherer to agricultural lifestyles has important implications for understanding the direction of causation. If domestication preceded the Neolithic agricultural revolution by several thousand years, as the Bonn-Oberkassel evidence implies, then dogs were integrated into mobile foraging societies well before sedentary food production emerged. This would suggest that the initial benefits of the human-dog relationship were orientated towards hunting assistance, camp defence, and possibly companionship, rather than the herding and livestock guarding roles that dogs are often assumed to have been domesticated to serve. The archaeological record from early agricultural sites, however, indicates a rapid diversification of dog morphologies coinciding with the spread of farming economies, suggesting that agricultural contexts drove a secondary phase of intensive selective breeding.

GThe study of dog domestication remains one of the most active frontiers in archaeogenomics. Each new high-coverage ancient genome sequenced from Pleistocene wolf or early dog remains has the potential to reorder the current consensus substantially, and the field has repeatedly surprised researchers with findings that challenge seemingly settled conclusions. What is beyond dispute is that the association between dogs and humans has persisted across an extraordinary diversity of ecological, cultural, and technological contexts — a record of mutual adaptation without parallel in the relationship between our species and any other animal.

Microplastics in the Marine Environment

ASince the term 'microplastics' was formally introduced into the scientific literature by marine biologist Richard Thompson and colleagues at the University of Plymouth in 2004, the body of research documenting their presence, distribution, and ecological consequences in the world's oceans has expanded at a remarkable pace. What Thompson's team initially identified as small plastic fragments of less than five millimetres in diameter accumulating on beach sediments has since been detected at virtually every depth and latitude of the marine environment, from the surface microlayer of tropical seas to the hadal sediments of the Mariana Trench, more than ten kilometres below sea level.

BMicroplastics enter the ocean through two principal pathways. Primary microplastics are manufactured at small sizes and enter the marine environment directly: microbeads used in personal care and cosmetic products, resin pellets lost during industrial transport and processing, and synthetic textile fibres released during domestic laundering. Secondary microplastics result from the fragmentation of larger plastic items — packaging, fishing gear, agricultural films — through ultraviolet radiation, mechanical abrasion by waves, and the combined action of thermal cycling and oxidative degradation. Estimates vary considerably, but a widely cited meta-analysis published in Nature Communications suggests that between 4.8 and 12.7 million metric tonnes of plastic waste entered the ocean annually during the period 2010 to 2015, with synthetic fibre inputs alone accounting for a substantial proportion of the total microplastic load in coastal surface waters.

COnce in the marine environment, the distribution of microplastics is governed by a complex interplay of physical and biological processes. Surface currents concentrate particles in the subtropical gyres — most prominently in the North Pacific, where the so-called Great Pacific Garbage Patch has attracted widespread public attention, though the actual density of particles within it is considerably lower than popular accounts often suggest. Vertical transport mechanisms, including biofouling — the colonisation of particle surfaces by microorganisms that increases their density — and incorporation into marine snow, carry significant quantities of microplastics from the surface to the seafloor. Polar regions, long assumed to be relatively pristine, have been found to accumulate microplastics transported by thermohaline circulation, with recent surveys recording concentrations of up to 12,000 particles per litre in Arctic sea ice cores.

DThe ecological impacts of microplastic ingestion have been documented across a wide range of marine taxa. In zooplankton such as copepods, laboratory studies have demonstrated that ingested polystyrene microspheres reduce feeding activity, impair reproductive output, and alter buoyancy regulation — effects that, if replicated at ecologically realistic concentrations in the field, would have significant implications for the efficiency of the biological carbon pump, since copepods play a central role in the vertical transfer of organic matter to depth. Fish exposed to microplastic particles under experimental conditions exhibit altered predator avoidance behaviour and reduced growth rates, though extrapolating laboratory findings to field populations requires caution given the substantially higher concentrations typically employed in experimental protocols relative to field measurements.

EA dimension of microplastic contamination that has attracted increasing scientific attention is the role of plastic particles as vectors for the transport of chemical contaminants and invasive microorganisms. Plastic surfaces sorb persistent organic pollutants — including polychlorinated biphenyls and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons — from surrounding seawater, potentially at concentrations several orders of magnitude above ambient levels. When ingested by marine organisms, these contaminants may be transferred to biological tissues, raising concerns about their bioaccumulation through marine food webs. Separately, the surfaces of microplastic particles have been found to harbour biofilms of diverse microbial communities, including potentially pathogenic bacteria such as members of the genus Vibrio, as well as non-native species capable of being transported across oceanic biogeographic boundaries — a phenomenon researchers have termed the 'plastisphere'.

FQuantifying the full ecological and human health implications of microplastic contamination remains a significant scientific challenge. A fundamental problem is the heterogeneity of the microplastic category itself: particles vary in polymer type, size, shape, surface chemistry, and degree of weathering, and these properties substantially influence their biological interactions. A further complication arises from dose-response uncertainty: while numerous laboratory studies have demonstrated adverse effects, the exposure concentrations used typically exceed those currently measured in most marine environments, making risk extrapolation to real-world conditions technically problematic. The European Food Safety Authority and equivalent bodies in several other jurisdictions have concluded that current evidence is insufficient to establish definitive safe thresholds for human dietary exposure to microplastics via seafood consumption.

GEfforts to address microplastic contamination have operated at both regulatory and technological levels. Several national governments have enacted bans or phase-outs of microbeads in rinse-off cosmetic products — a relatively tractable source — while the broader challenge of secondary microplastic generation from plastic waste is addressed, with varying degrees of ambition and effectiveness, through extended producer responsibility schemes, plastic tax mechanisms, and investment in improved waste management infrastructure. Emerging technologies for microplastic removal from wastewater effluent, including electrocoagulation and magnetic separation techniques, show laboratory-scale promise but have not yet been demonstrated at operational scale in full municipal treatment systems. The scientific consensus is that no single intervention will be sufficient, and that meaningful reductions in ocean microplastic loads will require coordinated action across the full plastic production and waste management lifecycle.

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy: Mechanisms and the Debate over Efficacy

ACognitive behavioural therapy, commonly abbreviated to CBT, occupies a dominant position in the evidence base for the psychological treatment of a broad range of mental health conditions, including depression, generalised anxiety disorder, panic disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, and obsessive-compulsive disorder. Its widespread adoption by national health systems — most prominently the United Kingdom's Improving Access to Psychological Therapies programme, which was explicitly predicated on the availability of randomised controlled trial evidence for CBT's effectiveness — reflects a consensus view among regulatory and clinical bodies that it is among the most rigorously evaluated and reliably effective forms of psychological intervention currently available. That consensus, however, has not gone unchallenged, and a substantial body of critical scholarship has raised significant questions about the quality of the supporting evidence, the theoretical coherence of the model, and the durability of treatment gains in real-world clinical populations.

BThe theoretical foundations of CBT were developed across several decades, drawing on Aaron Beck's cognitive model of depression, first articulated in the 1960s, and the behaviour therapy tradition deriving from learning theory and conditioning research. Beck's central proposition was that emotional disorders are maintained by systematically distorted patterns of thinking — what he termed 'cognitive distortions' — and that therapeutic change could be achieved by identifying these distortions, subjecting them to empirical evaluation through Socratic questioning, and replacing them with more accurate appraisals. Subsequent theoretical elaborations, including Albert Ellis's rational emotive behaviour therapy and the second-wave emphasis on behavioural activation as a primary mechanism of change in depression, have broadened the model without fundamentally revising its core constructivist premise that beliefs mediate the relationship between events and emotional responses.

CThe empirical evidence for CBT's efficacy rests primarily on a large body of randomised controlled trials, many of which demonstrate statistically significant superiority over waiting-list controls or treatment-as-usual comparators across a range of diagnostic categories. Meta-analyses conducted by Stefan Hofmann and colleagues at Boston University and independently by Pim Cuijpers's group at the Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam have reported effect sizes in the moderate-to-large range for CBT compared with control conditions for both anxiety disorders and depression. The translation of these findings into clinical policy has been largely uncontested by mainstream psychiatry, and CBT is currently recommended as a first-line or second-line treatment in clinical guidelines published by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, the American Psychological Association, and comparable authorities in Australia, Canada, and the majority of European Union member states.

DA persistent methodological concern, however, is that the apparent superiority of CBT in many trials may be substantially attributable to allegiance effects — the well-documented tendency for therapy outcome research to favour treatments that are theoretically aligned with the investigators' own orientation. A comprehensive analysis by Dragioti and colleagues, published in Epidemiology and Psychiatric Sciences in 2017, examined 247 meta-analyses of psychological therapies and found that investigator allegiance was the single strongest predictor of reported effect size, accounting for a greater proportion of variance than the treatment modality itself. When studies conducted by researchers with no declared commitment to CBT were analysed separately, the advantage over bona fide alternative therapies — rather than passive controls — diminished substantially, a finding that the CBT research community has contested on methodological grounds but has not, in the view of critics, convincingly refuted.

EA second line of criticism targets the theoretical mechanism proposed to account for CBT's effects. The cognitive mediation hypothesis — the claim that CBT works specifically because it modifies dysfunctional cognitions, and that cognitive change is the proximal cause of symptomatic improvement — has proven surprisingly difficult to establish empirically. Several dismantling studies, which compared full CBT protocols with versions from which the putative active cognitive components had been removed, found that the abbreviated versions produced equivalent outcomes to the full treatment, suggesting that the cognitive restructuring component may not be causally necessary. Furthermore, studies using sequential mediation designs have found that mood improvements often precede rather than follow changes in cognitive appraisals, directly inverting the temporal sequence that the cognitive mediation model predicts.

FThe question of treatment durability has also attracted critical scrutiny. Although CBT trials typically demonstrate good maintenance of gains at six- and twelve-month follow-up assessments, the evidence base for longer-term outcomes is considerably thinner, and several longitudinal naturalistic studies have reported relapse rates in treated populations that are difficult to reconcile with the claim that CBT produces durable cognitive change. Jonsson and Hougaard's review of long-term outcomes in CBT for panic disorder, for example, found that while initial response rates were high, a significant proportion of patients who had remitted at post-treatment assessment met diagnostic criteria again at five-year follow-up — a pattern more consistent with symptom suppression than with the acquisition of the generalised coping skills that CBT's theoretical model holds to be its primary therapeutic vehicle.

GThe debate over CBT's mechanisms and limitations does not, on its own, constitute a basis for dismissing its clinical value. A treatment can be genuinely beneficial without its theoretical account of that benefit being correct, and the practical question of whether a given patient is likely to experience relief from distress is at least partially separable from the academic question of why that relief occurs. The most productive response to the critiques outlined above is arguably not to abandon CBT but to invest in the kind of precision medicine approaches — identifying which patients benefit most, under what conditions, and through what actual mechanisms — that would allow treatment matching to improve substantially. What the evidence collectively suggests is that CBT is a useful tool with real limitations, and that treating it as a panacea, as health system commissioning decisions in several countries have arguably done, risks both overstating its benefits and crowding out investment in alternative approaches with potentially distinct clinical utility.
